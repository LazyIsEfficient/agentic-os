# Sizing Fallacies — The Audit Before You Total

Most sizing numbers fail not in the arithmetic but in the assumptions feeding it. These are the recurring errors. Sweep for every one before you present a total; each has detonated a deck in a board meeting.

## The 1%-of-a-huge-market fallacy

"It's a $50B market and we only need to capture 1%." The tell of someone who has no plan to win a single customer and is hiding it behind a percentage that sounds modest.

- **Why it's wrong:** market share is *earned* customer by customer through a real go-to-market motion. It is never *assumed* as a fraction of a big number. The 1% is reverse-engineered to produce an exciting revenue figure, not derived from anything real.
- **The fix:** build SOM bottom-up from your actual funnel — reachable buyers × conversion × ramp — or anchor it to a comparable entrant's *actual* first-three-year trajectory. If you can't say *which* customers make up your share and how you reach them, you don't have a number.
- **The diagnostic question:** "Name the first 100 customers and how each one hears about us." If that can't be answered, the 1% is fiction.

## Double-counting

The same revenue counted twice, inflating every layer above it. Two flavors:

- **Across segments:** the same buyer appears in two segments you then sum (e.g. "SMBs" and "retail" overlap; a small retailer is in both). Reconcile to **distinct payers** before totaling.
- **Across stack layers:** counting revenue at two levels of the same value chain (e.g. total platform GMV *and* the take-rate revenue you'd earn on it, summed). Pick the layer your business actually monetizes and count only that.

**The fix:** before summing, ask "could a single dollar of spend, or a single buyer, be sitting in two of these buckets?" Build segments to be mutually exclusive, or explicitly subtract the overlap.

## Demand-on-paper

Treating projected, surveyed, or analyst-forecast demand as confirmed. Survey "would you buy this?" enthusiasm does not convert to purchases; analyst hockey-sticks are extrapolations, not orders.

- **The fix:** weight evidence by behavior. Actual spend (yours or a competitor's) > stated intent with deposit > stated intent alone. Discount paper demand heavily and label it as projected. (See `demand-validation.md`.)

## Survivorship bias

Sizing a market by studying only its visible winners and inferring demand is easy and plentiful — while the graveyard of entrants who found the demand wasn't there is invisible and uncounted.

- **The fix:** deliberately look for the failures in the category. Why did they die? If several died on the *same* wall (reach, willingness to pay, retention), your TAM may be real but your SAM or SOM is the trap.

## TAM-as-the-answer

Leading with (or stopping at) TAM because it's the biggest, most flattering number. TAM only tells you the room is worth entering; it says nothing about what you'll get.

- **The fix:** every TAM must be carried down to SAM and SOM. A sizing that goes quiet after TAM is a sales pitch, not an analysis. Decisions live in SOM.

## False precision

"$4.237B" implies a measurement you did not make and invites confidence you have not earned.

- **The fix:** report a **range** bounded by the conservative and optimistic settings of the load-bearing assumptions, and name those assumptions. A defensible range beats a precise fiction.

## The audit checklist (run before totaling)

1. Is any buyer or dollar counted in two segments or two stack layers? (double-counting)
2. Is any share figure a *picked percentage* rather than a *built funnel*? (1%-fallacy)
3. Is any input survey/forecast demand treated as confirmed? (demand-on-paper)
4. Did I infer demand only from winners? (survivorship)
5. Does the number stop at TAM? (TAM-as-answer)
6. Is the output a false-precise point instead of a reasoned range? (false precision)
7. Is every input cited, modeled-with-stated-assumption, or tagged `ASSUMED`?

Any "yes" to 1–6, or any "no" to 7, means the number is not ready to present.
