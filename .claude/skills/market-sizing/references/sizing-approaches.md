# Sizing Approaches — Top-Down vs Bottom-Up vs Value-Theory

Three ways to arrive at a market size. They are not interchangeable; each has a confidence profile and a characteristic failure. Run at least two and triangulate.

## Top-down

Start from a large published figure (analyst report, industry stat, government data) and carve it down to your slice with successive percentages.

- **How:** `category market size × % relevant × % reachable × …`
- **Strengths:** fast, cheap, anchors order of magnitude, easy to source headline numbers.
- **Failure mode:** the carve-down percentages are usually guesses dressed as data, and the starting figure often includes adjacent categories you don't actually serve. Top-down numbers drift high because every percentage is chosen optimistically. This is the natural home of the 1%-of-a-huge-market fallacy.
- **Use when:** you need a quick order-of-magnitude gut check, or to sanity-bound a bottom-up number. Never the number you stake a plan on.

## Bottom-up

Build the market from the unit of demand up: count reachable buyers, multiply by what each pays.

- **How:** `# of buyers in a defined segment × units per buyer × price per unit`, summed across segments (de-duplicated).
- **Strengths:** highest confidence, because it forces you to name *who buys, how often, at what price*. Exposes whether you actually understand the customer. Directly ties to a GTM motion.
- **Failure mode:** labor-intensive; sensitive to the buyer-count and price assumptions; segment overlap causes double-counting if not reconciled.
- **Use when:** anything you'll act on. This is the default and the number you defend. Prefer it whenever you can credibly count buyers.

## Value-theory (value-based)

Size by the *value created* for the customer and the share of that value you can capture in price — used when no clean comparable or unit price exists yet (new category, novel pricing).

- **How:** estimate the economic value your solution creates per customer (cost saved, revenue enabled, risk avoided), apply a defensible value-capture rate (the share customers will pay), then multiply by reachable buyers.
- **Strengths:** the only honest approach for genuinely new categories where there's no incumbent price to copy. Grounds price in willingness-to-pay rather than a number pulled from air.
- **Failure mode:** value-capture rate is highly uncertain and easy to inflate; "value created" can be theoretical rather than felt. Most fragile of the three — treat its output as a hypothesis to validate, not a fact.
- **Use when:** pricing is undefined and no comparable exists. Pair with demand validation; an unvalidated value claim is the weakest possible foundation.

## Triangulation — the discipline that makes any of them trustworthy

One method is a guess; two that converge is a defensible range.

1. Run **top-down** and **bottom-up** independently — do not let one inform the other while building, or you've laundered one assumption through two methods.
2. **Compare.** Convergence (within ~2–3×) → you have a credible range; report it. Divergence (>3×) → you've found the load-bearing disagreement. *That gap is the most valuable output* — it points at the assumption to interrogate (usually buyer count, price, or category definition).
3. **Resolve to a range, not a point.** Bound the range by the conservative and optimistic settings of the load-bearing assumptions. State which assumptions move it.
4. When pricing is novel, add **value-theory** as a third leg to stress-test whether the price in your bottom-up is even achievable.

The goal is never a single precise number. It's a range you can defend and a clear statement of the one or two assumptions the whole thing hangs on.
