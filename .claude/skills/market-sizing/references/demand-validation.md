# Demand & Opportunity Validation

Sizing assumes demand exists. **Validate that it does before you multiply anything** — a precise size on imaginary demand is precisely wrong. Validation answers two questions: *is the demand real?* and *is it reachable by us?*

## Is the demand real?

Real demand means a felt problem that someone already pays to solve — with money, time, or a workaround. Evidence, strongest to weakest:

1. **People pay for it today** — directly, to a competitor or incumbent. Strongest signal; the market is proven, the question is share.
2. **People pay for a substitute** — duct-tape solutions, spreadsheets, manual labor, an adjacent tool bent to the job. The problem is felt and budgeted; you'd be a better solution, not a new need.
3. **Stated intent backed by behavior** — searches, waitlists, pre-orders, switching inquiries. Weaker (intent ≠ purchase) but directional.
4. **Stated intent only** — survey "would you use this?" Weakest. People say yes to hypotheticals and don't buy. Discount heavily.

If the only evidence is level 4, you have a *hypothesis*, not a market. Either find level 1–3 evidence or flag the size as resting on unvalidated demand.

**The substitute test:** if there is no current solution *and* no substitute *and* no spend, the likely reason is the problem isn't painful enough to pay for — not that you've found virgin territory. Greenfield is usually a graveyard. Treat "no competition" as a warning, not a green light.

## Is the demand reachable?

A real market you can't reach is worth zero to you. Reachability gates (these become the SAM gates in the sizing model):

- **Channel** — can you get in front of these buyers through channels you can actually operate and afford?
- **Willingness to pay** — at *your* price, not just in principle. Demand at $10 can be zero at $100.
- **Switching cost / lock-in** — are buyers trapped by incumbents, contracts, integrations, or habit?
- **Eligibility** — regulatory, compliance, geographic, or platform barriers that hard-exclude buyers.
- **Timing** — is the buyer ready now, or are you early to a market that doesn't exist yet? Being right but early is indistinguishable from being wrong.

## Common validation errors

- **Demand on paper.** Treating survey enthusiasm or analyst projections as confirmed demand. Behavior > stated intent, always.
- **Confusing interest with willingness to pay.** "That's cool" is not "here's my card." Validate the price point, not just the appeal.
- **Survivorship bias.** Studying only the winners in a category and inferring easy demand, ignoring the graveyard of entrants who found it wasn't there.
- **Founder demand.** "I want this, therefore a market wants this." Your own need is sample size one.
- **Mistaking a feature for a market.** Real pain, but too narrow or too cheaply solved-adjacent to sustain a standalone business — surfaces as a collapsed SAM downstream.

## Output of validation

Before sizing, produce a one-line verdict: **demand is [proven / substituted / intent-only / absent]**, reachable via **[channel]**, gated by **[the binding constraint]**. If demand is intent-only or absent, say so loudly — it changes the confidence on every number that follows, and may itself be the no-go.
