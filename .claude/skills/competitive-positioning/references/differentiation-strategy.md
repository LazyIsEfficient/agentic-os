# Differentiation Strategy & Choosing a Position

The candidate open positions come from the [positioning matrix](positioning-matrices.md). This is where you choose one, test that it's defensible, and name what you give up. Choosing means abandoning ground — a position that wins every quadrant wins none.

## Differentiation must be defensible, not merely true

A true-but-copyable difference is a difference with a shelf life of one quarter. Run every candidate through the defensibility test:

| Source of defensibility | Why it holds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Network effects** | Each user makes it more valuable; rivals start from zero. | A marketplace, a collaboration graph. |
| **Switching cost** | Leaving is expensive (data, integrations, workflow). | A system of record. |
| **Proprietary data / model** | The asset can't be cloned by shipping a feature. | A dataset only you accumulate. |
| **Unwanted wedge** | A segment or use case the incumbent *won't* serve without cannibalizing itself. | Going down-market under an enterprise leader. |
| **Brand / category ownership** | You defined the category; rivals reinforce you by comparison. | Being the verb. |

A "faster / cheaper / nicer UI" difference appears on none of these rows. If your candidate only clears "true," it is a feature, not a position.

## Category framing chooses the battlefield

Which category you claim decides who you're compared to and which features read as essential. This is the strongest and most-missed move:

- **Compete in the incumbent's category** → you're measured on their criteria, on their home ground. You lose by default.
- **Reframe the category** → you change which features count. "We're not a cheaper CRM; we're a revenue-intelligence platform" moves the whole comparison set. The matrix axes shift, and the incumbent's strengths may become irrelevant.
- Category reframes must be **credible to the customer** — a category nobody recognizes is a category nobody buys. Reframe toward language customers already use, not invented jargon.

## Choosing — and conceding

1. **Pick the position** where a defensible difference meets a customer-relevant buying axis with open ground. That intersection is the slot.
2. **Name what you concede.** State explicitly which axes you are *handing to competitors*. "We will not be the cheapest; we will not serve teams under 10." Concession is what makes the position sharp and the trade-offs honest — it is the positioning equivalent of a strategy's "what we say no to."
3. **Pressure-test it.** Would a rival object if you claimed it (if not, it's not yours)? Can a competitor copy it in a quarter (if yes, it's not defensible)? Does a specific customer want it (if not, it's a clever, empty quadrant)?

## Common traps

- **Positioning against features, not jobs.** Customers buy outcomes; feature parity is a constraint, not a position.
- **Positioning for everyone.** A position aimed at the whole market lands with no one. Sharpen the "who."
- **Borrowing the leader's frame.** Competing on the incumbent's terms is choosing to lose; reframe the category instead.
- **Confusing a value prop with a position.** A value prop is what you do for a customer; a position is that *relative to the alternatives*. Positioning is always comparative.
- **Drifting into messaging.** The moment you start polishing the wording, you've left this skill. Output the position and its rationale; hand the words to `marketer`.

## Output

The chosen position, the defensibility source, the buying axis it wins on, what is conceded, and the pressure-test results — feeding the [positioning statement](../assets/positioning-statement-template.md).
