# Competitive Landscape Mapping

The goal: enumerate the *full alternative set* the customer chooses among — not just the obvious rivals. Most positioning fails here, by mapping only the three named competitors and missing the one that actually steals the deal.

## The four tiers

Map every competitor into exactly one tier. The tier determines how you compete against it.

| Tier | Definition | Example (project-tracking tool) | How you compete |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | Same category, same job, same buyer. Customer is explicitly comparing you side-by-side. | Jira, Asana, Linear | Differentiation on buying axes; you must reach parity on table stakes. |
| **Indirect** | Different category, overlapping job. Solves part of the same problem differently. | A spreadsheet, a Kanban whiteboard, GitHub Issues | Reframe the category; show the job is bigger than their tool covers. |
| **Substitute** | Not a product at all — a workaround, a process, a different budget line. | Email threads + a weekly standup | Quantify the cost of the workaround; make the pain legible. |
| **Do nothing** | The status quo. The customer keeps living with the problem. | "We just remember it" | Create urgency; the enemy is inertia, not a rival. |

"Do nothing" wins more deals than any named competitor in most markets. Always list it.

## Finding the non-obvious competitor

The dangerous competitor is rarely on the analyst's quadrant. Find it by asking:

1. **"What did the last lost deal switch to — or stay on?"** Often a substitute, not a rival.
2. **"What budget does this purchase come out of?"** Whatever else competes for that budget is a competitor, even from a different category.
3. **"What would the customer build or cobble together themselves?"** In-house builds and tool-chains are substitutes that don't show up in market reports.
4. **"What does the champion have to argue against internally?"** That objection names the real alternative.

## Scoping discipline

- **Cap the direct tier at ~5.** Beyond that you are listing the market, not the competitive set. Pick the ones customers actually name.
- **Each entry needs a reason it's in its tier.** "Competitor because big" is not a reason. "Competitor because it appears in 60% of our lost-deal notes" is.
- **Re-scope on entry, not on calendar.** A new entrant or a substitute that suddenly got cheaper changes the map; a quarter passing does not.

## Output

A tiered list: each competitor, its tier, one line on the job it does for the customer, and one line of evidence that it belongs in the set. This list feeds the teardown (which alternatives to investigate deeply) and the matrices (who to plot).
