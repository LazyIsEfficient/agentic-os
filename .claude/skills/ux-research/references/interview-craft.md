# Interview Craft

A user interview is harder than it looks. The mechanics — sit down, ask questions, take notes — are easy. The craft — getting honest, useful, *unbiased* answers from someone who has every social incentive to be polite — is the part that takes years to learn.

This file is the operating guide. Read it before your first interview. Re-read it before your tenth.

## The Stance

The interviewer's job is **to listen, not to talk**. The participant should be doing roughly 80% of the talking. If you're talking more than 20% of the time, the interview is becoming a sales conversation, a feature pitch, or a confirmation exercise. Stop and let them speak.

The mental model: you are a curious anthropologist visiting a foreign culture. Your job is to *understand*, not to evaluate. Your opinions are interesting to nobody in the room, including (especially) you.

This stance is hard. The instinct of every product person is to:

- Defend the product when criticized.
- Pitch a feature when an opportunity arises.
- Steer toward the question that will validate the team's plan.
- Fill silence with explanations.

All four are interview-killers. Train yourself out of them.

## Before the Interview

### Read the protocol

You wrote the discussion guide; now read it again, out loud, in the order you'll ask. Notice which questions feel awkward; smooth them out.

### Decide what you want to learn

Beyond the discussion guide, what are the *three things* you most want to walk away knowing? Write them at the top of your notes. They're your fallback when the conversation drifts.

### Set the room

For in-person: a quiet, comfortable space. Water available. Don't sit across a desk; sit at a corner if possible — less adversarial. Have a clock visible to you, not to them.

For remote: test the call link beforehand, on the same device you'll use. Have a backup link ready (Zoom and Google Meet, e.g.). Test screen share if needed. Be in a space without interruptions.

### Get consent on the record

Once recording starts, restate consent on the recording: "Just to confirm, you're okay with us recording this conversation, using it internally for product research, and you can ask to stop at any time?" The participant says yes (or no, or asks questions). This protects you and respects them.

## The Interview Structure

A 45–60 minute interview has roughly four sections:

| Section | Duration | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Warmup | 5 min | Build rapport, set expectations, ease the participant in |
| Context-setting | 5–10 min | Understand who they are, what they do, what their world looks like |
| Key questions | 25–35 min | The main subject of the interview |
| Closing | 5 min | Wrap up, give them the floor, thank them |

Don't sprint into the key questions. The first 10–15 minutes are an investment in the quality of the next 30.

### Warmup

Begin with friendly, low-stakes conversation. Not "tell me about your job" but "thanks so much for making time today — how's your morning going?" or similar. Re-state the goals of the conversation in plain terms: "We're trying to understand how people like you handle X. There are no right or wrong answers; we're just trying to learn."

Two things to mention up front:

1. **There are no wrong answers.** Especially important when the participant might worry about making the interviewer happy.
2. **You can interrupt me, ask questions, or pass on anything.** Gives them agency; loosens the interviewer/participant dynamic.

### Context-setting

Before diving into the topic, get a feel for the person:

- "Tell me a bit about what you do."
- "What's a typical day look like for you?"
- "What does your team look like?"

This isn't filler. It calibrates everything that comes after — when they later say "we use Slack for everything," you'll know whether "we" means a team of 3 or 300.

### Key questions

The main event. Ask the questions in your discussion guide, but be willing to follow the conversation. The discussion guide is a fallback, not a script.

The single most important technique here is **asking about specific, recent, real situations** — not hypotheticals.

| Bad question (hypothetical) | Better question (specific, recent, real) |
|---|---|
| "How would you use a feature like this?" | "Tell me about the last time you tried to do something like this. Walk me through what you did." |
| "Would you pay for this?" | "When was the last time you paid for software? What pushed you to do it?" |
| "How important is collaboration to you?" | "Tell me about the last time you collaborated with someone on a project. How did it actually work?" |
| "What features do you wish you had?" | "What's the most recent thing that frustrated you in your workflow?" |

People are bad at hypothetical questions. They give you the answer they think you want, or the answer that sounds good, or the answer they aspire to. Specific past events are the only reliable signal of behavior.

### Closing

Wind down gently. Two important questions to ask near the end:

- **"Is there anything I should have asked you that I didn't?"** Often produces the best material in the entire interview. Participants spend the first 50 minutes answering your questions; in the last 5, they get to volunteer what they thought was actually important.
- **"Is there anyone else you'd recommend I talk to?"** Snowball-sample the next interview.

Then thank them, confirm compensation, end the recording, walk them out (or end the call). Don't keep talking after the recording stops without permission — anything they say off the record is off the record.

## The Probe Ladder

The single most important interviewing skill is *probing*: getting beyond the first answer to the real one. People answer in layers, and the first layer is almost never the most useful one.

Three kinds of probe, in increasing depth:

### 1. Echo / paraphrase

Repeat the participant's last few words or paraphrase what they said. Invites them to continue without you steering.

> Participant: "...so I usually just give up and do it manually."
> Interviewer: "You give up and do it manually."
> Participant: [continues with the *why* — which is the gold]

This feels awkward at first because it sounds like nothing. It's not nothing; it's the most powerful interviewer move there is. Used well, it produces 30 seconds of additional, unprompted material.

### 2. Open follow-up

Ask a question that invites elaboration without leading.

- "Tell me more about that."
- "What was that like?"
- "Walk me through what happened."
- "What were you thinking when you did that?"
- "What was going on around you at the time?"

These are not interesting questions on paper. They are devastatingly effective in practice.

### 3. Specific deepening

Once you have a story, ask about the *concrete details*: time, place, people, tools, words. The details are where the insight lives.

- "Where were you when this happened?"
- "Who else was involved?"
- "What did you say to them?"
- "How long did it take?"
- "What did you do next?"

The combination of "tell me about the last time" + the probe ladder + specific deepening produces interviews that feel almost magical compared to question-and-answer interviews. Practice this until it's reflexive.

## Silence

The single most underused tool in interviewing.

When a participant finishes a sentence, **wait**. Not 1 second — count to 5 in your head. Often they will continue, and what they say next is more honest, more nuanced, or more emotional than what they said first.

Silence feels uncomfortable, especially to the interviewer. The interviewer wants to fill it; the participant is happy to wait. **Resist filling it.** Look down at your notes. Take a sip of water. Make space for them to keep going.

The same principle applies after a question: ask the question, then *stop*. Don't elaborate, don't restate, don't explain what you mean. Let the participant interpret the question and answer it. If they need clarification, they'll ask.

## Asking About the Negative

Participants instinctively soften criticism, especially of products made by people sitting across from them. Compensate:

- **Frame the negative as helpful**: "We'd love to hear what *didn't* work — that's the most useful kind of feedback."
- **Ask about specific moments of frustration**, not general satisfaction: "Walk me through a time when this got in the way."
- **Normalize complaints**: "We've heard from a few people that this part is confusing — does that match your experience?"
- **Don't defend.** When they say something negative, *listen*. Do not explain why the design decision was made. Do not pitch the next version. Just listen and probe for more.

When you receive criticism well, the participant gives you more of it. When you receive it defensively, they shut down for the rest of the interview.

## Common Biases — and How to Fight Them

### Leading questions

The most common interview failure. A question that contains its own answer.

| Leading | Neutral |
|---|---|
| "How easy was it to find the dashboard?" | "Walk me through what you'd do to find the dashboard." |
| "Don't you find this a bit confusing?" | "What's going through your head as you look at this?" |
| "Was the new version better?" | "Tell me about your experience with the new version." |
| "How much do you love this feature?" | "How do you feel about this feature?" |

Leading questions feel natural when you're invested in a product. Train yourself to hear them in your own draft and rewrite them.

### Closed vs open questions

| Closed (yes/no) | Open |
|---|---|
| "Do you use the dashboard?" | "Tell me about how you use the dashboard." |
| "Was that frustrating?" | "What was that experience like?" |
| "Are you happy with the response time?" | "How does the response time fit into your work?" |

Closed questions get short answers. Open questions get stories. You want stories.

### Confirmation bias

You will hear what you expect to hear. The defenses:

- **Write down the hypothesis before the study** so you can be honest with yourself about whether the data confirms or refutes it.
- **Look actively for contradicting evidence**. After each interview, ask: "what did I hear that *doesn't* fit my hypothesis?"
- **Have someone else review your synthesis**. Their fresh eyes will catch your blind spots.

### Social desirability bias

Participants tell you what they think makes them look good. They will overestimate their own diligence, deny embarrassing behavior, and exaggerate their use of features they think you want them to use.

Defenses:

- **Ask about behavior, not opinion.** "When was the last time you did X?" is harder to fudge than "do you think X is important?"
- **Normalize the embarrassing thing.** "Lots of people skip this step — what do you usually do?"
- **Compare with peer behavior.** "What do you think your colleagues do?" — people are sometimes more honest about others.

### Recency bias

The last thing they did is the easiest to remember and the hardest to weigh accurately. Don't generalize from one recent event.

### Acquiescence bias

People say yes to be polite. If you ask "do you like X?" and they say "yes," that's information about politeness, not about X. Ask open questions; observe behavior; look for inconsistencies.

## Note-Taking and Recording

### Recording

**Always record (with consent)**, even if you also have a notetaker. Memory is unreliable; the recording is the source of truth during synthesis.

- Use two methods (phone + dedicated recorder, or cloud + local) — you will lose at least one recording over the course of a year.
- Test the recording works *before* the participant arrives. The worst feeling is realizing 30 minutes in that nothing was captured.
- Auto-transcribe (Otter, Rev, Whisper) but plan time to clean up the transcript — auto-transcription is ~85% accurate, which is the wrong level for direct quoting.

### Notes

Take light notes during the interview. Heavy note-taking pulls your attention away from the participant and away from listening. The recording will be there for the details.

Useful things to note in real time:

- **Quotes** that capture something especially well, with timestamps.
- **Observed behavior** (frustration, hesitation, switching tabs to check something).
- **Things to follow up on** later in the same interview.
- **Things you want to remember to add to the next interview** — questions you wish you'd asked.

After the interview, take 10-15 minutes to write a "fresh memory" summary while it's still in your head. This is where the real notes live.

## Solo vs Paired

Pairs (interviewer + notetaker) are usually better than solo:

- **The interviewer** focuses entirely on the conversation.
- **The notetaker** captures details, watches body language, and tracks time.
- **The two debrief immediately** after, each contributing what they noticed.

For remote interviews, the notetaker can be off-camera. For in-person, they sit slightly behind or to the side of the interviewer to keep the social dynamic dyadic.

Paired interviews are also a great way to train new researchers — they shadow as notetaker for several sessions before running their first interview.

## Anti-Patterns

- **The interview that's actually a sales pitch.** "Have you considered our new feature?" The participant is now in a sales conversation.
- **The interview that's actually a focus group.** Two participants, talking to each other, both performing for the room. Use focus groups intentionally; don't let them happen by accident.
- **The interrogation.** Question, answer, question, answer. No probes, no follow-ups, no stories. Surface-level answers; no insight.
- **Defending the design.** Participant criticizes; interviewer explains why it was built that way. Participant shuts down; interview is dead.
- **Filling silence.** Participant pauses; interviewer rushes in with the next question. The participant was about to say something more honest. You missed it.
- **Asking about features in a discovery interview.** "Would you use this?" Wrong tool, wrong phase.
- **Talking 50% of the time.** The interviewer learns nothing; the participant feels lectured at.
- **Note-blind interviewing.** Heavy note-taking; eye contact gone; participant feels like a data source. Lighter notes, more listening, more recording.
- **Skipping the warmup.** Diving into key questions before rapport is built. Shallow answers for the first 20 minutes.
- **No recording.** Trusting memory. Memory is wrong.
- **Off-the-record material treated as data.** They said it after the recording stopped because they trusted you not to use it. Don't use it.
- **Asking about hypothetical futures.** "If we built X, would you...?" Polite agreement that doesn't predict behavior.
