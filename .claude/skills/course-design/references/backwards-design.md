# Backwards design (Understanding by Design)

Outline in reverse. Start from what the learner must be able to *do*. Only once you know that, design the evidence that proves it, and only then the activities that produce the evidence. Most courses fail because they invert this — they start with "topics to cover", then retrofit exercises, then discover the outcomes don't quite match. Backwards design prevents that drift.

## Three stages

### 1. Identify desired results

From the course brief, restate the learning outcomes as observable actions the learner can perform. Resist listing topics; a topic is a noun, an outcome is a verb phrase.

- **Topic (bad)**: "Prompting techniques."
- **Outcome (good)**: "Rewrite a vague prompt into a task brief with explicit context, constraints, and success criteria."

Group outcomes into modules by natural prerequisite chains. Each module should have one to two outcomes. More than that and the module is a course in disguise.

### 2. Determine acceptable evidence

For each outcome, ask: *what would I accept as proof that the learner can do this?* The answer is your assessment, and it should exist before the activity.

Forms of evidence, from lightest to heaviest:
- **Formative check**: a single question or micro-task at the end of a lesson. Answers "did this stick?"
- **Lesson exercise**: the learner produces a small artifact. Answers "can they do it once with training wheels?"
- **Module mini-project**: the learner composes several lessons' skills into one artifact. Answers "can they do it when the lesson isn't holding their hand?"
- **Capstone**: the learner solves a novel problem using the whole course. Answers "is this real now?"

Match the evidence type to the assessment bar stated in the brief. A no-summative brief doesn't get a capstone; a portfolio brief doesn't get only quizzes.

### 3. Plan learning experiences

Now, and only now, design activities. Each activity is in service of producing the evidence from stage 2. If an activity doesn't produce evidence for a stated outcome, cut it — it's a tangent.

The order within a lesson follows the worked-example progression:
1. Concrete hook — why this matters.
2. Core concept stated plainly.
3. Worked example — the author does the thing start to finish, narrating the reasoning.
4. Faded practice — the learner does it with partial scaffolding.
5. Independent practice — the learner does it from scratch.
6. Formative check — proof the outcome stuck.

## Anti-patterns this catches

- **Topic-driven outlines.** "I have ten topics, let me make them ten lessons." Each lesson lacks an observable outcome, so the assessment is retrofitted and shallow.
- **Assessment theater.** A quiz that asks learners to recall definitions when the outcome was "design a system". The evidence doesn't match the claim.
- **Activity-first design.** "Let's do a project on X!" The activity exists before anyone defined what completing it would prove. Projects designed this way often produce artifacts that nobody knows how to grade.
- **Outcome inflation.** Stating ten outcomes per module because "more is better". Learners retain fewer outcomes when more are promised; pick the load-bearing ones.

## Handoff to the lesson spec

Each lesson spec's "Primary objective" is a stage-1 result. Its "Formative check" is stage-2 evidence. Its "Worked example plan" and "Exercise progression" are stage-3 experiences. If a lesson spec has stages 1 and 3 but stage 2 is thin, the lesson will feel instructive but leave the learner unsure whether they got it — the most common authoring failure.
